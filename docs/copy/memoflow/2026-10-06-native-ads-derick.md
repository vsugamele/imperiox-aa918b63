```
I'm 67 now. Turns out my brain was never impaired.

It was locked out.

I taught high school chemistry for 31 years. Balanced equations in my head faster than the kids could punch them into a calculator. My wife called me the family encyclopedia. Not as a joke.

Then the small words started going. "Spatula." "Mailbox." My granddaughter's middle name.

I'd stand in the garage holding a screwdriver with no idea what I went out there to fix.

I don't blame either of them. They had fifteen minutes and a textbook that says memory fades with age. Nobody gets paid to ask what's in your well water.

The night it got real was a Thursday. 9:42 PM.

I was helping my grandson with his chemistry homework. Chemistry. And I couldn't remember what Cd stood for on the periodic table.

I'd known that table for 48 years.

He said, "It's okay, Grandpa," and went to ask his mom. He hasn't asked me since.

Two weeks later I told that story to Walt at church. Walt ran the lab at the county water plant for 26 years. He didn't laugh.

"You still on that well out on Route 9?"

"Since '88."

He pulled a napkin over and wrote two things. Cd. 48.

"Cadmium. Atomic number 48. Old pipes, fertilizer runoff, half the wells in this county. Your body's got no way to get rid of it, Ray. It just keeps it. For decades."

Then he said the thing I haven't stopped thinking about.

"Your brain runs on sugar. Insulin is the key that lets it in. That stuff doesn't touch the key. It rusts the lock."

The rusted lock.

We had a deadbolt on the back porch that seized up after twenty Ohio winters. Key still fit perfectly. Wouldn't turn. I spent a whole Saturday jiggling it before I figured out the key was never the problem.
```

## DK-02 · G3 · "The note in the freezer"

```
Last Wednesday I found a yellow sticky note in the freezer. Folded in half. Soft from the frost, stuck to a bag of peas.

It was my husband's handwriting. Shaky, all capitals, the way he writes now.

"LINDA'S BIRTHDAY. 14TH. DON'T FORGET."

I'm Linda. My birthday was three weeks ago.

He did forget. I'd told myself it didn't matter. Then I stood there with the freezer door open, holding a note he wrote so he wouldn't hurt me, hidden where I'd never find it, and I cried until the fridge started beeping.

We've been married 46 years. Frank was the one who remembered everything. Our anniversary. The kids' shoe sizes. The name of every waitress at the diner on 4th Street.

Now he asks me what day it is four, five times before lunch. Last spring he got lost driving home from the hardware store he's been going to since 1983.

I took the car keys. I hid them in my purse like a criminal.

I'm not proud of the next part.

For two years I told him to try harder. Write it down, Frank. Pay attention, Frank. I bought a $39 whiteboard for the kitchen. Puzzle books. Fish oil, ginkgo, the $72 bottle from the TV ad. His doctor put him on Aricept, then added Namenda. I kept a chart.

His memory got worse anyway. On all of it.

I'm not angry at his doctor. She's a good woman with a waiting room full of people just like us and about twelve minutes for each one. What else was she supposed to say?

But nobody tells you what it does to the one who stays.

I sleep in two-hour pieces. At 3:10 one morning last month I found him at the front door in his robe with the keys in his hand. He'd found my purse.

I stopped going to the church potluck. Our friends stopped calling. Not mean. Just gone.

In August our daughter took me to tour a memory care place. Nice carpet. Fish tank in the lobby. $7,850 a month. I sat in the car afterward and couldn't turn the key.

It was my hairdresser, of all people, who changed it.

Denise has cut my hair for 19 years. Her mother went through this. I told her about the note in the freezer and she stopped cutting.

"Has anybody ever tested Frank for metals?"

I didn't know what she meant.

"Mom's neurologist never did either. It was a nurse at her kidney doctor's who asked. Cadmium. It builds up in you for decades. Water, food, the cigarette smoke from forty years ago. Your body can't flush it on its own. It settles in and blocks the fuel."

Then she said, "You know how your kettle gets that white crust on the bottom? You never see it happen. You just notice one day it takes forever to boil."

The Scale. That's what I call it now.

Forty-some years of it settling in Frank's head, one day at a time, and not one test we ever paid for was looking for it.

That's why the whiteboard didn't work. That's why the puzzles didn't work. You can't scrub a kettle by boiling more water in it.

Denise texted me an article that night. A woman wrote it. She took care of her mother for six years, then caught herself checking her own memory. It explains what she found about the Scale, and what she does about it now.

I read it at the kitchen table at 1 AM with Frank asleep down the hall. Then I read it again.

If you're the one in your house who keeps the sticky notes, read it before you tour anywhere.
```

```
It can't. Not because you're not trying.
```
